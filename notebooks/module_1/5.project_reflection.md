# Project Reflection

The clinical question that interests me most is whether unsupervised learning can identify meaningful subgroups of critically ill patients who may share the same general diagnosis but have very different physiological profiles and clinical outcomes. I am particularly interested in sepsis because it is a highly heterogeneous condition. Two patients diagnosed with sepsis may differ substantially in their vital signs, laboratory abnormalities, severity of illness and eventual outcomes. My project gives me an opportunity to investigate whether these differences can be captured directly from Electronic Health Record data rather than relying only on traditional diagnostic labels. The broader goal of the capstone is also to determine whether the patient groups identified through clustering are stable, reproducible and associated with meaningful clinical outcomes.

The study by Ju and Dubin has been especially useful in shaping how I think about this problem. Their analysis used the multicenter eICU database to identify subpopulations of patients with sepsis through a combination of dimensionality reduction and density based clustering. They used demographic and admission information, laboratory measurements and longitudinal vital signs collected during the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Laboratory variables were summarized using minimum, maximum, mean and standard deviation while temporal patterns in heart rate, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation were represented using functional principal components. The authors then compared t-SNE, UMAP and VAE representations with DBSCAN clustering and used additional methods such as Self-Organizing Maps and outcome based analyses to evaluate the resulting groups. What I learned most from this study is that finding clusters is only one part of the problem. A clustering solution is more convincing when similar patterns appear across different methods and when the resulting groups have understandable clinical characteristics.

I anticipate that data quality and preprocessing will be among the greatest challenges in this project. ICU data contain large amounts of missing information, repeated measurements, irregular measurements frequencies and differences in how information is recorded across hospitals. The Ju and Dubin study also emphasizes that missingness is not necessarily random. For example, patients who receive more laboratory testing may be more severely ill, meaning that measurement frequency can contain clinical information of its own. Another challenge will be transforming longitudinal ICU data into patient level features without removing important information about changes over time. Feature scaling, categorical encoding, missing data handling and dimensionality reduction can all influence the structure seen by a clustering algorithm. I will therefore need to keep the preprocessing pipeline transparent and reproducible and evaluate whether conclusions remain consistent when reasonable analytical choices are changed.

For me, the discovered clusters would become clinically useful if they represent more than mathematically separated groups. I would want the clusters to show clear and interpretable differences in physiological characteristics, laboratory profiles, severity or patterns of organ dysfunction. I would also want them to differ in important outcomes such as ICU mortality, hospital mortality, ICU length of stay or hospital length of stay. These types of outcome comparisons were important in the Ju and Dubin analysis and are also central to the goals of this capstone project. Most importantly, the clusters should be reasonably stable when evaluated using different methods or sensitivity analyses. If the same types f patient subgroups repeatedly emerge and those groups have meaningful clinical differences, then the results could provide a useful foundation for understanding sepsis heterogeneity and, eventually, for studying whether different patient subgroups may benefit from different approaches to monitoring or treatment.

Overall, I see this project as a balance between statistical methodology and clinical interpretation. My immediate goal is to reproduce the sepsis clustering framework from Ju and Dubin as faithfully as possible using the eICU data. Doing this carefully will help me understand each step of the clustering workflow before extending the analysis further. More broadly, I want the project to demonstrate not only that unsupervised learning can divide patients into groups, but that those groups can provide meaningful information about differences among critically ill patients.
