## Derivation, Validation, and Potential Treatment Implications of Novel Clinical Phenotypes for Sepsis

#### What are they trying to solve ?

The main problem is that sepsis is extremely heterogenous.

Two patients may both be diagnosed with sepsis but have very different :

- organ dysfunction
- Laboratory abnormalities
- inflammatory responses
- severity
- mortality risk
- and potentially response to treatment

The authors felt that treating all sepsis patients as one broad group can hide meaningful differences between patients.

Their main question was essentially :

>Can routine clinical information available when a patient first arrives at the hospital be used to discover distinct and reproducible types of sepsis patients ?

They had three major goals :

- Discover sepsis phenotypes from routine clinical data.
- Determine whether those phenotypes are reproducible and clinically meaningful including whether they have different biomarkers and outcomes.
- Investigate whether having different proportions of these phenotypes in clinical trials might affect the apparent benefit or harm of treatments.

They were not trying to predict :

> Will this patient die ?

Instead they were asking :

> Are there naturally occurring groups of sepsis patients with different clinical patterns ?

That makes it directly relevant to phenotype discovery with unsupervised learning.

#### What datasets did they use for derivation and evaluation ?

This paper does not have a traditional machine-learning "training/test" split.

A better way to describe it is :

> $\text{Derivation Cohort} \rightarrow \text{Validation Cohort} \rightarrow \text{External Clinical Cohorts/trials}$

##### A. SENECA Derivation Cohort

This was the dataset used to discover the clusters.

- 12 hospitals in the UPMC health system in Pennsylvania
- Years: 2010–2012
- Adults meeting Sepsis-3 criteria within the first 6 hours of hospital presentation
- 20,189 sepsis encounters
- 16,552 unique patients

This is essentially the paper's "training" or cluster derivation dataset.

##### B. SENECA Validation Cohort

Used to check whether similar phenotypes could be found again.

- Same 12-hospital system
- Years: 2013-2014
- 43,086 sepsis encounters
- 31,160 unique patients

The important difference is the time period. The authors derived the phenotypes in earlier data and checked them in later data. 

##### C.GenIMS Cohort

A separate prospective study:

- Severe community-acquired pneumonia with sepsis
- 28 sites
- 583 sepsis patients

This cohort was particularly useful because it contained detailed biomarkers of the host immune response.

##### D. Three Randomized Clinical Trials

They also tested their phenotypes in three existing sepsis trials:

- **ACCESS:** 1,706 patients
- **PROWESS:** 1,690 patients
- **ProCESS:** 1,341 patients

Total:

4,737 RCT participants

These trials allowed the researchers to investigate mortality and possible differences in treatment effects across phenotypes.

```

        Validation
─────────────────────────────
        ↓

        +

```

This is a major strength of the paper : they did not simply discover clusters in one dataset and stop.

#### What clustering techniques did they use?

Primary method: Consensus K-means clustering

This is different from simply running K-means once.

Conceptually:

```
Repeated clustering
        ↓
Check how consistently patients
are grouped together
        ↓
Consensus clustering
        ↓
Stable patient groups
```

The idea is to identify groups that are more robust than the output from a single K-means run.

They applied consensus K-means to the 29 clinical variables.

##### OPTICS was used beforehand

Before deciding on the clustering approach, they examined OPTICS — Ordering Points To Identify the Clustering Structure.

They used OPTICS plots to examine the structure of the data and help choose the clustering strategy.

OPTICS was therefore not the final phenotype clustering algorithm.

##### Latent Class Analysis was used for validation

After obtaining the phenotypes with consensus K-means, they independently used : Latent Class Analysis (LCA)

The purpose was to ask:

> If we use a completely different clustering framework, do we still get approximately the same patient groups ?

The answer was largely yes: LCA also supported a four-class solution with similar clinical characteristics. 

##### t-SNE was used for visualization

They also used `t-SNE`.

But this is important :

> They did not use t-SNE as their primary clustering algorithm.

They used t-SNE to visualize the multidimensional phenotype structure in two dimensions.

So the methods should be separated like this:

|              Purpose              |        Method         |
| :-------------------------------: | :-------------------: |
|   Examine clustering structure    |        OPTICS         |
|          Main clustering          |   Consensus K-means   |
| Alternative clustering validation | Latent Class Analysis |
|           Visualization           |         t-SNE         |

#### What preprocessing did they perform before clustering?

This part is especially relevant to your future pipeline.

##### Step 1 : Early time window

The main analysis used clinical information from the `first 6 hours after hospital presentation`.

For each clinical variable, they selected the most abnormal value observed during that window. 

This converts repeated EHR measurements into one patient-level value.

For example:

```
Patient's heart rate during first 6 hours:


92
98
110
125
104
↓

Most abnormal value
125
```

The exact direction of "most abnormal" depends on the clinical variable.

##### Step 2 : Examine distributions

Before clustering, the researchers examined:

- variable distributions,
- missingness,
- correlations between variables. 

##### Step 3 : Missing - data handling

They used Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations — MICE to handle missing clinical measurements.

So they did not simply drop every patient with missing data.

##### Step 4 : Transform non-normal variables

For variables with non-normal distributions, they used `log transformation`.

Conceptually:

```
Highly skewed variable
        ↓
Log transformation
        ↓
Less extreme / more manageable distribution
```

##### Step 5 : Examine Highly correlated variables

They evaluated correlations among variables.

For sensitivity analyses, they excluded highly correlated variables using rank-order statistics.

Examples of variables excluded in one sensitivity analysis included:

- sodium
- hemoglobin
- BUN
- ALT

They also conducted another sensitivity analysis excluding variables with high missingness such as:

- ESR
- CRP
- bands

##### Step 6 : Test a different observation window

Their primary window was **6 hours**.

They repeated analyses using a `12-hour EHR window` to check whether their clusters depended heavily on the original 6-hour decision.

The phenotypes remained similar. 

###### Preprocessing summary

```
Raw EHR measurements
        ↓
Select first 6 hours
        ↓
Take most abnormal measurement
        ↓
Examine distributions
        ↓
Examine missingness
        ↓
MICE imputation
        ↓
Log-transform non-normal variables
        ↓
Examine correlations
        ↓
Consensus K-means
```

One thing I would not claim from this paper is that they used standard scaling or Min-Max scaling before clustering. The main article clearly describes log transformation, imputation, correlation assessment, etc., but it does not clearly state a general scaling procedure for the clustering inputs.

#### How did they evaluate the clustering?

Their evaluation is much broader than simply calculating one metric.

##### Consensus matrix

They examined the consensus matrix heatmap.

This asks:

> Across repeated clustering runs, do the same patients consistently end up together?

Clear blocks in the matrix indicate stable clusters.

##### Consensus CDF

They examined the **Consensus Cumulative Distribution Function** and the relative change in the area under that curve.

This helped determine whether adding more clusters actually improved the solution.

They found little statistical benefit in moving from **4 clusters to 5 or 6 clusters**.

##### Cluster size

They considered whether the resulting groups had reasonable sizes.

They did not want a statistically possible solution made mostly of tiny, unstable groups.

##### Pairwise consensus

They required adequate pairwise consensus between cluster members `> 0.8`

This provided evidence that patients within the same phenotype were consistently grouped together. 

##### Alternative clustering method

They repeated phenotype derivation using `Latent Class Analysis` and examined :

- Bayesian Information Criterion — BIC

- group size

- probability of group membership

- entropy

- clinical characteristics

Entropy remained greater than 0.8, supporting good classification.

This independently supported the four-class solution. 

##### Temporal validation

They re-derived the phenotypes in the later `SENECA 2013–2014 validation cohort`

Similar phenotype numbers, proportions, and characteristics appeared again.

##### External validation

They checked whether the phenotypes appeared in:

- GenIMS
- ACCESS
- PROWESS
- ProCESS

##### Sensitivity analysis

They checked whether the solution changed after:

- removing highly missing variables,
- removing highly correlated variables,
- using a 12-hour window instead of 6 hours.

The results remained broadly stable.

##### Biological validation

They compared the clusters against 27 host-response biomarkers.

This asks something very important:

> Are these just mathematical groups, or do they actually correspond to different biological states?

They found substantial biomarker differences between phenotypes.

##### Clinical outcome validation

They compared:

- in-hospital mortality,
- 28-day mortality,
- 365-day mortality,
- ICU admission,
- mechanical ventilation,
- vasopressor use.

They also used log-rank tests for mortality curves.

##### Important distinction 

They did **not primarily evaluate clustering using**:

- Silhouette score
- Davies–Bouldin Index
- Calinski–Harabasz Index

Instead, their focus was:

**stability + reproducibility + biological validity + clinical validity.**

That is an important lesson from this paper.

##### What results did they produce ?

The major result was the discovery of four sepsis phenotypes :

###### α — Alpha

$33\%$ of patients

The largest and generally least severe group.

Characteristics:

- fewer abnormal laboratory values,
- less organ dysfunction,
- lower vasopressor use,
- lower mortality.

###### β — Beta

$27\%$ of patients

Characteristics:

- older patients,
- more chronic illness,
- greater comorbidity burden,
- greater renal dysfunction,
- higher BUN and creatinine.

###### γ — Gamma

$27\%$ of patients

Characteristics:

- stronger inflammatory response,
- higher inflammatory markers,
- higher temperature,
- lower albumin,
- more pulmonary dysfunction.

###### δ — Delta

$13\%$ of patients

This was the smallest but highest-risk group.

Characteristics:

- elevated lactate,
- hypotension,
- elevated liver enzymes,
- liver dysfunction,
- septic shock,
- more severe organ dysfunction. 

So:

```
α → relatively mild
β → older / chronic illness / renal
γ → inflammatory / pulmonary
δ → shock / liver / metabolic / highest risk
```

#### Did the clusters differ clinically?

Yes very clearly. This is probably the most important finding of the study.

##### Mortality differed dramatically

n the SENECA derivation cohort, cumulative 28-day mortality was approximately:

| Phenotype | 28-day mortality |
| :-------: | :--------------: |
|     α     |      **5%**      |
|     β     |     **13%**      |
|     γ     |     **24%**      |
|     δ     |     **40%**      |

That is a huge separation in clinical risk.

The δ phenotype had roughly eight times the 28-day mortality of $α$ in the derivation cohort. 

In-hospital mortality also differed:

| Phenotype | In-hospital mortality |
| :-------: | :-------------------: |
|     α     |          2%           |
|     β     |          5%           |
|     γ     |          15%          |
|     δ     |          32%          |

Mortality differences across phenotypes were statistically significant, and the δ phenotype consistently showed the worst survival across multiple cohorts and trials.

##### Biological differences also existed

The phenotypes differed in biomarkers related to:

- inflammation,
- coagulation,
- endothelial dysfunction,
- renal injury.

Of the 27 biomarkers, 23 differed significantly across phenotypes in at least one study.

So the clusters were not simply mathematical divisions of routine laboratory values.

They also corresponded to different biological patterns.

##### They were not just severity groups

The researchers specifically investigated whether:

> α = low severity
>  β = medium severity
>  γ = higher severity
>  δ = severe

would explain everything.

It did not.

The phenotypes overlapped across SOFA and APACHE severity levels and differed in organ patterns and biomarkers.

Similarly, they were not simply:

- pneumonia group,
- urinary infection group,
- bloodstream infection group, etc.

So the phenotypes captured something more multidimensional than traditional diagnosis or severity alone.

#### What limitations did the study have?

The authors explicitly describe five major limitations. 

##### Limitation 1 - Only routine EHR variables were used

The phenotype derivation relied on routine clinical information.

They did not include information such as:

- clinicians' impressions,
- protein biomarkers,
- immune-cell gene-expression data,
- pathogen characteristics

during the original clustering.

Adding these richer biological variables might produce different phenotypes.

Their clusters describe:

> **clinical phenotypes**

not necessarily the full biological complexity of sepsis.

##### Limitation 2 - Several methodological decisions were subjective

Even though clustering was unsupervised, researchers still made important decisions beforehand.

For example:

- choosing a 6-hour observation window,
- selecting the 29 candidate variables,
- deciding how variables were transformed,
- selecting the optimal number of clusters.

Changing those assumptions could change the clusters. 

This is a very important lesson:

> Unsupervised learning does not mean "no researcher decisions."

There are still many choices before the clustering algorithm runs.

##### Limitation 3 — Missing data

Several clinical variables had substantial missingness.

The authors used multiple imputation, which introduces assumptions about what the missing values might have been.

They tried to address this by removing high-missingness variables in sensitivity analyses and still obtained similar results.

But missing data remained a limitation.

##### Limitation 4 — Outcomes differed across validation datasets

The cohorts were not identical.

They differed in things such as:

- sepsis definitions,
- patient demographics,
- severity,
- organ dysfunction.

Therefore, mortality and prognosis were not exactly the same across datasets.

This complicates direct comparisons of phenotypes across cohorts.

##### Limitation 5 — Initial phenotype discovery came from one health system

The original phenotypes were derived from:

**one integrated US health system — UPMC.**

Although they later reproduced them in other datasets, the initial derivation still represents one healthcare environment and one period in time.

The authors specifically said further validation was needed in:

- low- and middle-income countries,
- more recent clinical trials,
- longitudinal datasets. 

So they did not claim that Alpha/Beta/Gamma/Delta are universally established sepsis phenotypes that should already be used clinically.

#### Main Lesson :

The strongest methodological lesson from this paper is that finding clusters is only the beginning. Seymour et al. made their result convincing because they asked whether the clusters were:

```
stable → reproducible → biologically different → clinically different → associated with outcomes.
```

That is much stronger evidence than simply reporting that K-means produced four clusters. 

## Unsupervised Identification of Sepsis Subpopulations in the eICU Database

This paper is particularly relevant to your capstone because, unlike the Seymour paper, it uses the same eICU Collaborative Research Database that your project uses, and it focuses on measurements from the first 24 hours of the ICU stay.

### What are they trying to solve ?

The central problem is again heterogeneity among sepsis patients.

Patients with sepsis can differ substantially in:

- physiology,
- laboratory abnormalities,
- comorbidities,
- severity,
- response to treatment,
- and mortality.

The authors wanted to determine whether unsupervised learning could identify natural subpopulations of sepsis patients within the large, multicenter eICU database.

There was also a second methodological problem they wanted to address.

Earlier research had identified sepsis subgroups using MIMIC-III, which comes primarily from a single hospital. eICU contains patients from more than 200 hospitals, making it much more heterogeneous.

So their question was essentially:

> **Can clinically meaningful and stable sepsis subpopulations be discovered in the more heterogeneous eICU database, and do different unsupervised-learning methods identify similar structures?** 

They also wanted to compare several dimensionality-reduction methods rather than trusting a single representation.

Their overall idea was:

```
eICU sepsis patients
        ↓
Clinical features
        ↓
Different dimensionality reduction methods
        ↓
DBSCAN clustering
        ↓
Compare clusters across methods
        ↓
Determine whether clusters are stable
and clinically meaningful
```

### What dataset did they use for clustering and evaluation?

The study used the eICU database, containing ICU data collected from more than 200 hospitals across the United States.

The initial cohort contained 23,136 ICU admissions identified as sepsis patients.

Sepsis patients were identified using the diagnostic field in the eICU `patient` table.

#### Cohort filtering

The researchers then applied several exclusions.

Starting population :

```
23,136 sepsis ICU admissions
```

Excluded :

- 3,191 who were not first hospital stays
- 845 who were not first ICU stays/readmissions
- 1,057 patients younger than 18
- 283 cardiothoracic surgical ICU admissions
- 166 admissions missing demographic information
- 1,530 without vital-sign records in the first 24 hours
- 9,131 without sufficient selected laboratory information

##### Final Cohort

6,934 admissions / 6,934 unique patients

Average age:

```
65.2 years
```

Male patients:

```
3,564 — approximately 51.4%
```

The cohort-selection flowchart on page 2 of the paper clearly shows this reduction from 23,136 admissions to 6,934 final patients. 

#### Was there a training and test dataset?

Not in the traditional supervised-machine-learning sense.

There was no structure like:

```
70% training
30% testing
```

because this was an unsupervised clustering study.

The same eICU cohort was used to discover clusters, while robustness was evaluated using:

- different dimensionality-reduction methods,
- different missing-data strategies,
- different hyperparameter settings,
- SOM validation,
- cluster agreement,
- and clinical outcomes.

They also compared their findings with an earlier MIMIC-III sepsis clustering study, but that was a cross-study comparison rather than a conventional held-out test set.

### What variables did they use from eICU?

This section is extremely important for your project.

Their clustering features came primarily from:

- demographics,
- admission information,
- laboratory measurements,
- vital signs.

Most physiological and laboratory information focused on the **first 24 hours of the ICU stay**.

#### Demographic and admission variables

They included:

1. Age
2. Gender
3. Ethnicity
4. Admission height
5. Unit type
6. Admission weight

These describe basic patient characteristics and ICU context.

#### B. Laboratory variables

They selected **12 laboratory measurements**:

1. Bicarbonate
2. Lactate
3. Potassium
4. Platelets
5. Anion gap
6. Chloride
7. Blood urea nitrogen — BUN
8. Creatinine
9. Sodium
10. Glucose
11. White blood cell count — WBC
12. Hemoglobin — Hgb

But they did something more sophisticated than selecting a single laboratory value.

For every laboratory variable, they calculated four statistics during the **first 24 hours**:

- Minimum
- Maximum
- Mean
- Standard deviation

So, for example, lactate became approximately:

```
Lactate
   ↓
minimum lactate
maximum lactate
mean lactate
standard deviation of lactate
```

With 12 lab variables × 4 summaries, that creates **48 laboratory features**.

#### Number of laboratory measurements

They also included:

```
Total number of laboratory measurements during the first 24 hours.
```

This is an interesting feature.

The researchers found that patients who received laboratory tests more frequently tended to have higher mortality.

That means:

> measurement frequency itself may contain information about illness severity.

A severely ill patient may be tested more frequently than a stable patient.

So instead of treating measurement frequency as irrelevant, they explicitly used it as a feature. 

#### D. Vital signs

They used three major vital signs:

1. SpO₂ — oxygen saturation
2. Heart rate
3. Respiratory rate

These were measured during the **first 24 hours of ICU admission**.

But unlike the laboratory variables, they did not simply calculate minimum/maximum values for these time series.

They tried to preserve their temporal behavior.

#### E. Functional Principal Components for vital signs

The vital signs could be recorded approximately every five minutes.

So instead of reducing them to one number, they used:

```
Functional Principal Component Analysis — FPCA/FPC
```

using the **PACE algorithm**.

Conceptually:

```
Heart rate over 24 hours


80
82
90
110
105
95
...
        ↓
Functional PCA
        ↓
Small number of features describing
the shape of the patient's HR trajectory
```

The same idea was applied to:

- heart rate,
- respiratory rate,
- oxygen saturation.

This allowed the clustering algorithm to capture some information about how physiology changes over time, rather than only static measurements.

#### Variables they did NOT use

An important point is that variables appearing later in their results are not necessarily clustering inputs.

For example:

- APACHE score
- ICU mortality
- hospital mortality
- ICU length of stay
- hospital length of stay

were primarily used to characterize or validate the discovered clusters, not as the main variables used to create them.

That distinction matters because otherwise you would effectively be clustering patients partly based on their outcomes.

### What clustering techniques did they use?

Their main clustering algorithm was Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)

However, DBSCAN was not applied directly to the full original feature matrix.

Their pipeline was:

```
Clinical variables
        ↓
Dimensionality reduction
        ↓
2-dimensional representation
        ↓
DBSCAN
        ↓
Patient clusters
```

They repeated this pipeline using different dimensionality-reduction methods.

#### Method 1 — t-SNE + DBSCAN

First `t-SNE` reduced the feature space to two dimensions.

Then `DBSCAN` identified dense groups of patients.

```
Features
   ↓
t-SNE
   ↓
DBSCAN
   ↓
Clusters
```

#### Method 2 — UMAP + DBSCAN

They repeated the process with:

**UMAP**

```
Features
   ↓
UMAP
   ↓
DBSCAN
   ↓
Clusters
```

UMAP attempts to preserve local neighborhood structure while also providing a useful low-dimensional representation.

#### Method 3 — VAE + DBSCAN

They also used a Variational Autoencoder — VAE to learn a latent representation of the patient data.

Then:

```
Features
   ↓
VAE
   ↓
Latent representation
   ↓
DBSCAN
   ↓
Clusters
```

#### Method 4 — SOM for validation

They additionally used **Self-Organizing Maps — SOM**

However, SOM was mainly used as a topological validation and visualization method, rather than being the main cluster generator.

The researchers projected the t-SNE, UMAP, and VAE cluster assignments onto the SOM U-matrix.

The workflow diagram on page 4 summarizes this structure:

```
Clinical features
    ↓     ↓     ↓
 t-SNE  UMAP   VAE
    ↓     ↓     ↓
 DBSCAN DBSCAN DBSCAN
      \    |    /
       comparison
           ↓
          SOM
           ↓
Clinical cluster analysis
```

### What preprocessing techniques did they use before clustering?

#### Step 1 — Patient cohort filtering

They restricted the dataset to:

- adults,
- first hospital stay,
- first ICU stay,
- non-cardiothoracic surgical ICU patients,
- patients with required demographic information,
- patients with usable vital-sign data,
- patients with selected laboratory data.

This reduced the cohort from:

```
23,136 → 6,934 patients.
```

#### Step 2 — First 24-hour window

Most physiological information was restricted to the **first 24 hours of the ICU stay.**

This is directly relevant to your project because your project also focuses on first-24-hour physiology and laboratory measurements.

#### Step 3 — Laboratory aggregation

Repeated laboratory values were converted into:

```
minimum
maximum
mean
standard deviation
```

for each patient.

For example:

```
Patient's creatinine measurements

1.2
1.5
1.7
2.0
        ↓

Min  = 1.2
Max  = 2.0
Mean = ...
SD   = ...
```

This converts a long-format laboratory table into patient-level clustering features.

#### Step 4 — Functional PCA for vital signs

Repeated:

- HR,
- RR,
- SpO₂

measurements were summarized using **functional principal components**.

This preserved information about their temporal trajectories.

#### Step 5 — One-hot encoding

Categorical variables such as:

- ethnicity
- unit type

were converted using **one-hot encoding.**

For example:

```
Unit type

MICU
SICU
Neuro ICU

        ↓

MICU    SICU    Neuro ICU
1       0       0
0       1       0
0       0       1
```

#### Step 6 — Min-Max scaling

They applied **Min-Max scaling to all variables.**

Conceptually:

```
Original variable
        ↓
rescale
        ↓
approximately 0 → 1
```

This matters because DBSCAN and the dimensionality-reduction methods depend heavily on distances.

Without scaling, a feature with a large numerical range could dominate the distance calculation.

#### Step 7 — Euclidean distance

They used **Euclidean distance** consistently for dimensionality reduction and clustering comparisons.

#### Step 8 — Dimensionality reduction

They reduced the high-dimensional feature space using:

- t-SNE
- UMAP
- VAE

before applying DBSCAN.

Their reasoning was that high-dimensional distance can become unreliable because of the curse of dimensionality, and dimensionality reduction can reduce noise and collinearity. 

#### Step 9 — Missing-data handling

Missingness was a major problem.

The authors did not simply impute everything automatically.

For extremely incomplete vital-sign information, they avoided imputation because they were concerned it might create artificial physiological patterns.

For sensitivity analysis, they tested several imputation methods.

##### Categorical variables

###### **Mode imputation**

##### Numerical variables

They compared:

- Median imputation
- K-nearest-neighbor — KNN imputation
- Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations — MICE

They then checked whether the clusters changed substantially after these different missing-data strategies.

The easiest way to remember it is:

```
    ↓
First 24-hour data
    ↓
Laboratory summaries
Min / Max / Mean / SD
    ↓

    ↓

    ↓

```

### What clustering evaluation techniques did they use?

#### Silhouette Score

Measures how similar patients are to their own cluster compared with other clusters.

Higher is better.

Their results were:

| Method         | Silhouette |
| -------------- | ---------- |
| t-SNE + DBSCAN | **0.67**   |
| UMAP + DBSCAN  | **0.84**   |
| VAE + DBSCAN   | **0.89**   |

#### Davies–Bouldin Index — DBI

Measures similarity between clusters.

**Lower is better.**

| Method         | DBI      |
| -------------- | -------- |
| t-SNE + DBSCAN | **0.25** |
| UMAP + DBSCAN  | **0.09** |
| VAE + DBSCAN   | **0.19** |

UMAP had the best DBI.

#### Calinski–Harabasz Index — CHI

Measures cluster separation relative to within-cluster variation.

**Higher is better.**

| Method         | CHI            |
| -------------- | -------------- |
| t-SNE + DBSCAN | **8,576.69**   |
| UMAP + DBSCAN  | **54,413.23**  |
| VAE + DBSCAN   | **138,637.94** |

#### Cross-method agreement

The researchers compared cluster membership between:

```
t-SNE ↔ UMAP
t-SNE ↔ VAE
UMAP ↔ VAE
```

using **contingency tables**.

They found:

##### t-SNE vs UMAP

###### **Strong agreement.**

Many patients were grouped similarly by the two methods.

##### VAE vs t-SNE/UMAP

###### **Much weaker agreement.**

VAE divided patients differently.

#### SOM topological validation

They projected the cluster assignments onto the **Self-Organizing Map U-matrix**.

The idea was:

> If the clusters are real and stable, their boundaries should correspond to natural boundaries visible in another representation of the data.

t-SNE and UMAP clusters aligned reasonably well with the SOM structure.

VAE clusters were much more dispersed.

This provided additional evidence that the t-SNE/UMAP structures were more robust. 

#### Adjusted Rand Index — ARI

For sensitivity analyses, they used **Adjusted Rand Index** to determine whether patient assignments remained similar after different missing-data treatments.

For t-SNE and UMAP : `ARI > 0.98`

compared with the complete-case analysis.

That indicates extremely high stability.

VAE was much more sensitive to how missing values were handled.

#### Hyperparameter robustness

They also varied DBSCAN parameters.

They tested approximately:

- ε from **3–7**
- MinPts from **40–60**

and different SOM settings.

Cluster membership remained largely stable with:

**ARI > 0.95.**

This suggests that their results were not simply caused by one very specific hyperparameter choice.

#### Clinical outcome validation

This was another major part of their evaluation.

They compared clusters using:

- ICU mortality
- Hospital mortality
- ICU length of stay
- Hospital length of stay
- APACHE score
- Kaplan–Meier survival curves

This helped answer:

> Are these mathematically different clusters also clinically different?

The answer was yes.

#### Important lesson from their evaluation

Interestingly, **VAE had the highest Silhouette and CHI**, yet its clusters showed poorer agreement with other methods and weaker separation of survival outcomes.

That means:

> **A high Silhouette score alone does not prove that a cluster solution is clinically meaningful.**

This is highly relevant to your capstone.

### Did the discovered clusters differ clinically?

 **Yes.**

The authors identified several recurring clinical patterns.

They did not name them Alpha/Beta/Gamma/Delta like Seymour et al., but they described recognizable physiological phenotypes.

#### Phenotype 1 — Severe metabolic / renal dysfunction

This was one of the clearest high-risk patterns.

Characteristics included:

- high lactate,
- high anion gap,
- high BUN,
- high creatinine,
- low bicarbonate,
- high variability in several measurements.

Clinically this suggests:

**metabolic acidosis + renal dysfunction + tissue hypoperfusion.**

Examples were:

- t-SNE Cluster 5
- UMAP Cluster 5
- VAE Cluster 5

In-ICU mortality was approximately:

- **20.9%** for t-SNE Cluster 5
- **20.9%** for UMAP Cluster 5
- **15.5%** for VAE Cluster 5

These patients also tended to have longer hospital stays.

#### Phenotype 2 — Renal / inflammatory-metabolic abnormalities

Another group showed:

- elevated sodium,
- elevated chloride,
- elevated creatinine,
- moderately elevated lactate,
- elevated BUN,
- relatively high APACHE scores.

The authors interpreted this as a pattern involving renal dysfunction, metabolic disturbance, and physiologic stress.

#### Phenotype 3 — Hypermetabolic / high-risk immune pattern

Another high-risk subgroup showed:

- markedly elevated glucose,
- elevated hemoglobin,
- lower WBC count.

Examples:

- t-SNE Cluster 14
- UMAP Cluster 13

These clusters had approximately:

**31% in-hospital mortality**

and APACHE scores around:

**81.83**

The authors interpreted the low WBC in the context of sepsis as potentially reflecting impaired host immunity, combined with severe metabolic stress.

#### Phenotype 4 — Relatively low-risk metabolic profile

Another subgroup had:

- lower glucose relative to other groups,
- relatively higher hemoglobin,
- relatively lower WBC,
- no strong lactate abnormality,
- no major BUN abnormality,
- no major creatinine abnormality.

Examples included:

- t-SNE Cluster 12
- UMAP Cluster 11

This group showed some of the **lowest mortality** in the cohort.

The authors interpreted it as a comparatively metabolically stable, lower-risk group.

#### Physiological features associated with mortality

The paper also examined which cluster-level characteristics correlated with in-hospital mortality.

Some important findings were:

###### Lactate

Positive correlation:

approximately 0.55–0.66

Higher lactate → higher mortality.

###### Bicarbonate

Negative correlation:

approximately −0.24 to −0.75

Lower bicarbonate → higher mortality.

###### BUN / Creatinine

Positive correlations around:

0.36–0.47

Higher renal dysfunction → higher mortality.

#### Survival differences

The authors also generated Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the clusters.

The plots on **page 10** showed that:

- t-SNE clusters had meaningful survival separation,
- UMAP clusters had meaningful survival separation,
- VAE clusters showed weaker separation.

So t-SNE and UMAP appeared more clinically interpretable even though VAE had excellent internal cluster metrics. 

### What limitations did the study have?

The authors identify several important limitations.

#### Limitation 1 — Large amount of missing data

This is probably the biggest limitation.

The eICU database had substantial missingness, especially in:

- vital signs,
- laboratory variables,
- medication-related information.

Because of this, many patients had to be excluded.

They started with:

**23,136 sepsis admissions**

and finished with:

**6,934 patients.**

That is a substantial reduction.

##### Why this matters

The final patients may systematically differ from patients who had incomplete measurements.

That creates potential selection bias.

#### Limitation 2 — Some physiologic variables could not be included

Many patients lacked sufficient first-24-hour vital-sign records.

Therefore, the authors could not include as many physiologic variables as they would have liked.

This limits the clinical richness of the clusters.

#### Limitation 3 — Some laboratory variables had to be excluded

There was a tradeoff:

```
Include more laboratory variables
             ↓
Lose many patients because of missing data
```

versus:

```
Use fewer laboratory variables
             ↓
Keep more patients
```

The authors chose a subset that maintained a reasonable sample size.

So the final clustering representation does not contain every potentially useful laboratory measurement.

#### Limitation 4 — Treatment variables were not included

Important interventions such as:

- mechanical ventilation,
- vasopressor therapy

were not incorporated into the clustering features.

The authors explain that treatment information was difficult to use because of:

- inconsistent units,
- inconsistent drug/brand representation,
- substantial medication-table missingness.

This means their clusters mainly characterize **patient physiology**, not complete treatment patterns. 

#### Limitation 5 — More than 200 hospitals create extra heterogeneity

The multicenter nature of eICU is a strength because it improves diversity.

But it is also a limitation.

Different hospitals can have different:

- patient populations,
- measurement practices,
- documentation,
- treatment practices.

Therefore:

```
200+ hospitals
      ↓
Better diversity
      +
More noise and inconsistency
```

The authors specifically note that this heterogeneity may make the results less directly applicable to decision-making at one individual hospital.

#### Limitation 6 — Dimensionality reduction loses information

The original clinical feature space was compressed into low-dimensional representations.

For example:

```
Many clinical features
        ↓
UMAP
        ↓
2 dimensions
```

Some information is inevitably lost during this process.

The authors acknowledge that clustering directly in a model-based high-dimensional framework might avoid part of this information loss.

#### Limitation 7 — Embedding dimensions are difficult to interpret clinically

A UMAP or t-SNE coordinate such as:

```
UMAP1 = 4.3
UMAP2 = -1.8
```

does not have a direct medical interpretation.

You cannot tell a physician:

> "This patient has UMAP1 of 4.3."

The researchers therefore had to interpret clusters afterward using the original variables.

Because of this, they caution that the clusters should be viewed as:

**data-driven representations**

rather than immediately established clinical phenotypes. 

#### Limitation 8 — No new external-dataset validation

The paper compared its findings with patterns reported previously in **MIMIC-III**, but it did not independently reproduce its entire pipeline in another new external ICU database.

The authors specifically recommend future replication in additional ICU datasets.

So their strongest validation is:

- cross-method consistency,
- SOM topology,
- missing-data sensitivity,
- hyperparameter stability,
- and outcome differences,

rather than full external replication.

### Biggest Lesson

This paper is especially useful because its research design is very close to your capstone: 

**eICU + first-24-hour physiology/labs + multiple clustering approaches + quantitative evaluation + clinical outcome comparison**.

The strongest lesson is not simply “UMAP + DBSCAN works.” It is that a convincing clustering analysis should evaluate the result from several angles:

**cluster separation + stability + agreement across methods + sensitivity to preprocessing + clinical interpretation + outcome differences.**

The VAE result demonstrates why this matters: it achieved an excellent **Silhouette score of 0.89**, yet showed weaker agreement with the other methods and weaker survival separation. A mathematically strong cluster solution is therefore not automatically the most clinically useful one.
